Leasing, Decoded: Three Numbers Build the Payment

A lease is a long-term rental with finance woven in: you pay for the slice of the car you use up (its depreciation), rent on the money tied up in it, and tax on both. Everything else — the capitalized cost, the money factor, the residual — is just the machinery that produces those three numbers. A quick example with modest figures: a $36,000 car, $3,000 down, no trade-in, a residual of $19,800 after 36 months, a money factor of 0.00175 (a 4.2% APR), and 6% tax. The adjusted capitalized cost is $33,000. Depreciation: ($33,000 − $19,800) ÷ 36 = $366.67. Finance charge: ($33,000 + $19,800) × 0.00175 = $92.40. Tax: ($366.67 + $92.40) × 6% = $27.54. Payment: $486.61 a month. Once you can rebuild a quote this way, no lease worksheet can hide anything from you.

The four inputs, and who controls each

Capitalized cost is the car's negotiated price — "cap cost" — plus any fees rolled in. You control it exactly as a buyer does, which is why experienced lessees negotiate the price first, as if buying, and mention the lease afterward. Down payments and trade-ins reduce it ("cap cost reduction"). Residual value is the financing company's forecast of the car's worth at lease end — and your guaranteed buyout price. The bank sets it; you cannot negotiate it, but you can choose cars that hold value, since a high residual means less depreciation to pay for. Money factor is the rate in disguise: multiply by 2,400 for the APR, and expect it to track your credit — weaker credit, higher factor. Term is usually 24–48 months; staying inside the factory warranty window is the quiet perk of the standard 36.

Mileage, wear, and the end-of-lease inspection

Leases cap miles — typically 10,000–15,000 a year, 12,000 most often — and charge per mile over, so a cheap payment on a low-mileage lease can become expensive for a long commuter; "high-mileage" leases cost more monthly but beat paying penalties. Buying extra miles upfront is cheaper than paying for them at turn-in, and buying the car at lease end erases the charge entirely. Wear and tear is judged at inspection: normal — small dings and scuffs, minor stains, routine tire and brake wear — costs you nothing. Excessive — broken or missing parts, frame or body damage, dead electronics, bald mismatched tires — is billed to you. A wash, scratch buffing, replacing small broken trim, and stain removal in the final week is money well spent; wear-and-tear insurance exists for the anxious. The contract also obliges you to service the car on schedule, with proof — skip it and penalties (and warranty trouble) follow.

Why lease — and the honest counterweights

The case for leasing: a lower monthly payment and less cash down for the same new car; a car that is always under warranty and in its most reliable years; in most states, sales tax only on the payments you make rather than the whole price (on the defaults above, about $1,456 of lease tax versus $3,500 to buy); an easy trial — love it and buy it at the preset residual, or hand back the keys; and for businesses, lease payments are generally deductible as an operating expense, which suits many self-employed drivers. The counterweights are just as real: at lease end you own nothing — no equity, ever; the car is the lessor's property, so modifications are restricted; mileage caps punish road trips and long commutes; and perpetual leasing means perpetual payments, while a buyer eventually owns years of payment-free driving. Getting out early

Life changes — a growing family, a longer commute, a tighter budget. The exits, in rough order of cost: ask the lessor for relief — a few months' deferral is sometimes granted, with the payments tacked on later. Transfer the lease — a swap service matches you with someone who takes over the same payment for the remaining term, for a few hundred in transfer fees, where the contract and state allow it. Buy the car out and then sell or keep it — sensible when the buyout is near the car's market value; note the buyout mid-lease is usually the residual plus remaining depreciation, not just the residual. And the plain early return — simplest, and usually priciest: an early-termination fee plus the remaining depreciation the lessor hasn't collected yet. Price the buyout against the car's real market value before choosing; in a strong used-car market it occasionally wins outright.

FAQ

What is a money factor, in plain terms?

The lease's interest rate in disguise. Multiply by 2,400 to get the APR — 0.00208 ≈ 4.99%. Always convert it before comparing with a loan.

Is leasing cheaper than buying?

Monthly, yes; across a lifetime of driving, usually no — you never stop paying. The same-term comparison above shows the lease's edge while it lasts.

What happens at the end of the lease?

Return the car (after inspection, plus any mileage/wear charges), buy it at the residual value, or lease/buy something new. Three doors — the residual makes the buy-it price known from day one.

Should I put a big down payment on a lease?

Cautiously. It lowers the payment, but if the car is totaled or stolen early, that upfront cash is generally gone — the insurer settles with the lessor. Many advisors keep lease down payments small for exactly this reason.
