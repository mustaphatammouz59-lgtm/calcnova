Why the order matters

At the defaults — $14,500 across three cards and a $500 budget — the avalanche plan finishes in 38 months with $4,471 of interest. Card 2 dies first (month 16) not because it is smallest but because its 19.99% APR is the priciest debt in the pile; its freed $280 then snowplows into Card 1 the very same month. Paying the cards in the wrong order — cheapest rate first — keeps expensive balances alive longer and quietly adds hundreds of dollars of interest.

Paying Off Several Cards at Once

Multiple cards are common — the average American carries more than two — and they can be genuinely useful: different rewards for different spending, more total available credit, a backup when one card is declined or stolen, and a lower credit utilization ratio, which helps credit scores. The cost of that convenience appears when balances revolve: several minimums, several due dates, and several APRs compounding at once. A payoff plan turns that pile back into one number — a single monthly budget, deliberately aimed.

The Debt Avalanche

The avalanche is the mathematically cheapest order. First, every card's contractual minimum is paid, protecting you from late fees and penalty rates. Whatever budget remains goes entirely to the card with the highest interest rate. When that card reaches zero, its entire payment — minimum and extra together — rolls down to the next-highest rate. Because each dollar of spare payment always works against the most expensive remaining debt, no other ordering of the same budget can produce less total interest. This calculator also assumes what the method requires: no new charges on any card, minimums and rates unchanged for the life of the plan.

The Debt Snowball

The snowball swaps the target: after minimums, spare money goes to the smallest balance, whatever its rate. It costs a little more interest, but it deletes whole cards sooner, and for many people that visible progress is the difference between a plan abandoned in month three and a plan finished. Toggle the method above and compare the two schedules on your own numbers — if the interest difference is small, choose whichever order you will actually stay with. The best plan is the finished one.

Managing the Pile While You Pay

Three habits protect the plan. Align the due dates — most issuers let you move them, so all payments can leave on one day. Automate at least the minimums so a busy month never becomes a late fee and a penalty APR. And stop charging the cards in the plan: a revolving balance usually cancels the grace period, so new purchases start accruing interest the day they are made. If a card's APR is the problem, a balance transfer or a lower-rate consolidation loan can cut the interest load — just compare the fee against the interest actually saved, and keep the same monthly budget aimed at the smaller pile.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which method is cheaper, avalanche or snowball?

Always the avalanche, by construction — spare dollars kill the most expensive debt first. The snowball trades a modest interest premium for faster small wins.

What if my budget doesn't cover all the minimums?

The plan can't start until it does: minimums are not optional. Raise the amount, cut a balance with a transfer, or ask issuers about hardship arrangements — the calculator flags this case instead of printing a fantasy schedule.

Why does a payment jump in the middle of the schedule?

That's the cascade: a finished card's payment rolls into the next target, sometimes mid-month when the payoff leaves change over. Each jump is the plan accelerating.

Should I close cards as they are paid off?

Usually not right away: closing reduces your total available credit and raises your utilization ratio, which can dent your score. Keep paid-off cards open and unused (or cut them up but leave the accounts), unless an annual fee makes closing worth it.
