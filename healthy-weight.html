What is a healthy weight?

The healthy range is computed from BMI. Given a height, the weights that produce a BMI of 18.5 to 25 define the healthy band; the same arithmetic gives the boundaries for every other category — below 18.5 underweight, 25–30 overweight, above 30 obese. An adult of 18 or older inside 18.5–25 is conventionally classed as healthy or normal weight. The full machinery is in our BMI Calculator — but one caveat travels with it: BMI is an estimate that is blind to body composition. It is calibrated to the average person, so it can mislabel the very muscular as overweight and the lightly-built as fine when they are not. Sorting yourself out properly takes body composition — and for a personal call, a medical professional.

Effects of being underweight, overweight, and obese

Underweight (BMI below 18.5)

People end up underweight through genetics, too little food, a fast metabolism, illness, or drug use. Persistently low weight is linked to conditions such as hyperthyroidism, cancer, and tuberculosis, and it can itself be a sign of an undiagnosed disease. Studies find mortality among the underweight rising to levels comparable with the morbidly obese — though some of that signal is disease causing the weight loss rather than the other way round, and underweight without an underlying illness may carry little extra risk. The day-to-day effects are clearer: low energy and stamina, osteoporosis, and a weaker immune system with more infections. Underweight from poor-quality food risks outright malnutrition, with physical and mental developmental consequences, and — in severe cases — conditions such as kwashiorkor and marasmus. For women, being underweight through an eating disorder can stop menstruation, impair fertility, and complicate pregnancy.

Overweight (BMI 25–30)

Overweight usually means consuming more energy than the body spends, though alcohol, genetics, eating disorders, inactivity, poor nutrition, and stress all push the same direction. Its harms are genuinely debated. Overweight is generally held to cause the same problems as obesity in milder form — yet several large studies, including Flegal and colleagues' analysis in JAMA, found this band associated with mortality no higher, and sometimes lower, than the healthy band. Some read that as evidence that 25–30 might be near-optimal; others answer that mortality is not the whole of health, and the band's elevated risks of diabetes, heart disease, and several cancers matter regardless.

Obese (BMI over 30)

Obesity is most often the product of too little movement, too much food, and a genetic susceptibility that makes both easier; rarer causes include endocrine disorders, medication, and mental illness. It is largely preventable through diet and activity — but "eat less, move more" is an answer for an individual, and at population scale the causes involve food environments and economics that no algorithm settles. The medical stakes are high: obesity sharply raises the risk of cardiovascular disease, stroke, hypertension, type 2 diabetes, osteoarthritis, several cancers, obstructive sleep apnea, and depression, and it measurably shortens life expectancy — which is why it ranks among the leading preventable causes of death worldwide.

Maintaining a healthy weight

Underweight, overweight, and obesity call for different directions of travel, but the levers are the same family: food, activity, and — where relevant — mental health. To gain weight healthily, eat more often and lean on nutrient-dense foods: whole grains, vegetables, lean protein, nuts and seeds. Resistance training adds the weight as muscle. When an eating disorder such as anorexia or bulimia is the cause, treatment has to address the psychological side alongside the physical one.

Coming down from overweight or obesity works through the same pair of levers. WHO guidance is to cut back on calorie-dense foods, total fats, and sugars while eating more fruit, vegetables, legumes, whole grains, and nuts, and to be regularly active — around 60 minutes a day for children and 150 minutes a week for adults. Individual effort works best where the environment helps: nutrition education, affordable healthy choices, less sugar and salt engineered into processed food, and less marketing of it to children.

FAQ

My weight is in the healthy range — am I definitely healthy?

Not automatically. The range is a screening tool built on population averages: fitness, body fat distribution, blood pressure, and blood work all carry information BMI can't see. Treat the range as a first check, not a certificate.

Does this apply to children?

No — the 18.5–25 band is for adults. Children are assessed against age-and-sex growth percentiles (the CDC charts), which is what our BMI Calculator uses for ages 2–20.
