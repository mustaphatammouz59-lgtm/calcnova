Social Security: The System Behind the Claiming Decision

Before 1935, caring for America's elderly and disabled was a family, municipal, or state affair. The Social Security Act of that year (signed by Franklin Roosevelt; first taxes collected January 1937) made it federal — retirement benefits at first, survivors' benefits for spouses and children from 1939, disability from 1956. Today the system (formally OASDI: Old-Age, Survivors, and Disability Insurance) is the main income source for most retired Americans, and the only source for a significant share — though it was designed to replace only about 40% of the average worker's pre-retirement income, based on the average of their 35 highest-earning years. About 169 million Americans pay in; about 65 million — one in five — collect; more than three in five beneficiaries rely on it for over half their income; administration costs roughly 1% of outlays.

The tax that funds it, and the tax on it

Social Security runs pay-as-you-go: today's workers fund today's beneficiaries through the 12.4% FICA payroll tax, split evenly between employee and employer (the self-employed pay both halves), on wages up to a cap that rises with inflation — $184,500 in 2026, making the maximum employee share $11,439 and the self-employed maximum $22,878; earnings above the cap are untaxed (one reason the tax is regressive, and why lower-wage earners receive higher relative benefits). Payroll taxes supply roughly 90% of revenue; the rest comes from interest on the trust funds' Treasury bonds and from taxing benefits themselves — since 1983, higher-income retirees pay income tax on up to 50% of benefits (combined income $25,000–$34,000 single / $32,000–$44,000 married) or up to 85% (above those bands); combined income means AGI plus nontaxable interest plus half of benefits, and Roth IRA withdrawals don't count. Of each FICA dollar, roughly 72¢ funds retirement benefits, 16¢ disability, 9¢ survivors, and under 1¢ administration. Exempt from the tax: certain religious groups, some state and local employees covered by their own public pension systems, nonresident aliens, and students working at their own school. Benefits get a yearly COLA tied to the CPI-W (third quarter to third quarter; no increase, no COLA) to protect purchasing power.

Full retirement age, the earnings test, and when to claim

Full retirement age is 67 for those born in 1960 or later, between 66 and 67 for births in 1943–1959 (survivor benefits use a different FRA; widows and widowers can claim from 60). Benefits can start at 62 — you may apply from 61 years 9 months, since the SSA processes applications up to four months early — reduced by 5/9 of 1% per month for the first 36 months early and 5/12 of 1% beyond that, or grow by 8% a year of delay past FRA until 70, after which delay buys nothing. Working while claiming before FRA triggers the earnings test: $1 of benefits is withheld per $2 earned above the annual limit (the withholding stops at FRA, whatever you earn). The ideal claiming age turns on cash needs, life expectancy, current earnings, marital status, and a spouse's age, income, and health — a struggling 62-year-old with a short horizon should claim; a healthy higher-earner with a longer-lived spouse often gains by waiting, since the delayed benefit becomes the survivor's too. If you claim and regret it, the SSA allows one withdrawal of the application within 12 months, provided every dollar is repaid. Eligibility itself needs credits: four a year at most, 40 in total (about ten years of work), each 2026 credit earned with $1,890 of taxed wages — $7,560 buys the year's maximum — and credits, once earned, never expire. Benefits can be received while living abroad (Medicare cannot), with tax returns still due.

Disability, spouses, and survivors

The SSA's disability standard is strict — total disability only: you cannot do your previous work, cannot adjust to other work because of the condition, and it has lasted or will last at least a year or end in death. SSDI pays after a five-month waiting period (severe cases can be fast-tracked by compassionate allowance) and requires 6–40 work credits depending on age — typically 40, with 20 in the last ten years; at FRA, disability benefits convert to retirement benefits at the same amount, and the two can't be collected together. SSI, funded from general revenue rather than FICA, serves those with limited means (one screen: $2,000 or less in cash and bank balances) regardless of work history, usually bringing Medicaid with it; some people qualify for both. Spouses 62+ can claim up to 50% of a working spouse's benefit once that spouse has filed; a widow or widower can take a survivor benefit from 60 if the marriage lasted over nine months (waived when caring for a child under 16), keeps the larger of the two benefits — never both — and can switch strategically between their own and the survivor benefit. Divorced spouses married 10+ years, now unmarried, whose own benefit is smaller, can claim on an ex-spouse's record from 62 — even if the ex hasn't filed yet, provided both are over 62.

FAQ

Why does the calculator use a $1,000 benefit?

Claiming-age rankings don't depend on the size of your benefit — every age's stream scales with your primary insurance amount — so the table is normalized to a $1,000 FRA benefit. To get your dollars, divide each figure by 1,000 and multiply by your actual FRA benefit from your SSA statement.

Why do the two break-evens in calculator 2 differ?

The classic break-even compares raw totals: the age at which the bigger late checks repay the checks you skipped. The present-value break-even also charges the late strategy for the investment return the early checks could have earned. At a 0% return the two coincide; the higher your return, the further apart they spread — and the more the early claim is favored.
