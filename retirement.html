Retirement: What It Is, How Much to Save, and Where the Money Comes From

To retire is to withdraw from active working life — usually for good, and usually somewhere between 55 and 70, though health, disability, job strain, or simple readiness can move the date either way, and some people semi-retire on reduced hours first. The gating question is rarely desire; it is whether retirement is financially possible at all. Retiring on Social Security alone is technically possible and distressingly common in the U.S., but the program was designed to replace only about 40% of the average worker's wages — a floor, not a lifestyle.

How much to save: three rules of thumb

There is no universally right number — it depends on the income you want, your health and life expectancy, other income, and even whether leaving an inheritance matters to you — but three guidelines frame most plans. The 10% rule: save 10–15% of pre-tax income every working year ($5,000–$7,500 on a $50,000 salary); at that pace from age 25, a $1 million nest egg by retirement is genuinely possible. The 80% rule: most retirees can maintain their standard of living on 70–80% of pre-retirement income — $70,000–$80,000 a year replacing a $100,000 salary — though the right figure swings with the retirement you picture, yacht or cabin. The 4% rule: divide the annual income you want by 4% to size the nest egg — $100,000 a year demands $2.5 million. Experts who prefer multiples land in the same neighborhood: roughly 15–25× current annual income. The first calculator above converts these abstractions into your numbers.

Inflation, the quiet tax

U.S. inflation has averaged roughly 2.6–3.5% over long horizons, which means a dollar 30 years from now buys what about 40 cents buys today — the single most common reason retirement needs get underestimated. Inflation is unpredictable and outside anyone's control, so most planning focuses on steady total return rather than on inflation itself; those who do want a hedge reach for Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS), commodities such as gold, real estate, or dividend-paying stocks over short-term bonds. The calculators here handle inflation the honest way: targets and withdrawals are inflated, so the numbers are in the dollars you'll actually spend.

Where retirement money comes from

Social Security is the base layer — a government insurance program funded by FICA payroll taxes, paying benefits loosely tied to past earnings but deliberately progressive: a $20,000 earner might receive ~$800/month, a $100,000 earner ~$2,000/month, so lower earners replace a larger share of their income. 401(k), 403(b), and 457 plans are the main private engine: employer plans, often with a matching contribution (say 3% of pay — $1,800 on a $60,000 salary; only about 6% of sponsoring companies contribute nothing), built from pre-tax dollars that grow untaxed until withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income, usually in a lower retirement bracket. Always contribute at least enough to capture the full match — it is an instant 100% return. IRAs apply the same shelter individually: traditional IRAs take pre-tax contributions and tax withdrawals; Roth IRAs invert it — after-tax money in, tax-free money out. Pensions, the pooled employer-managed funds once common in the private sector and still standard for many public servants, have faded as longevity rose and each retiree is supported by fewer workers; at retirement they pay out as fixed income or a lump sum convertible to an annuity.

Beyond the shelters sit ordinary investments — mutual and index funds, stocks, bonds, real estate, commodities, and CDs — used both inside the tax-advantaged accounts and, once their annual limits are maxed, outside them; and plain personal savings, essential as an emergency fund but a poor long-term retirement vehicle since liquid accounts rarely beat inflation after tax. Other sources fill specific gaps: home equity (including reverse mortgages, which pay retirees to stay in their homes until ownership transfers at the end), annuities — immediate (one premium, payments from next month) or deferred (an accumulation phase, then lifetime payouts) — passive income from rentals, businesses, dividends, or royalties, and inheritances, which may still carry estate, inheritance (six U.S. states levy their own), or capital-gains tax before they can fund anyone's retirement.

FAQ

Why does calculator 1 ask for "other income" in retirement-age dollars?

Because that's how those benefits are quoted — a Social Security estimate or pension figure is a promise in future dollars. The spending target, by contrast, is anchored to today's income and inflated forward, so both sides meet in the same dollars at retirement. Enter other income as the monthly amount you expect to actually receive at your retirement age.

What return should I assume?

Something honest for your asset mix: diversified stock-heavy portfolios have historically returned more than bond-heavy or cash ones, but with far deeper drawdowns on the way. Stress-test your plan at 2 points below your first guess — a plan that only works at optimistic returns isn't a plan yet.
