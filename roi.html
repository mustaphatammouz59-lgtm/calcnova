ROI: The Napkin Metric

Return on investment is finance's universal first pass: a single percentage that can be attached to anything with a cost and a hoped-for gain — stocks, real estate, employees, a sheep farm. Before any serious opportunity gets serious analysis, ROI is the base it stands on, and its survival in a world of fancier formulas is explained by its simplicity: two numbers anyone at the lunch table already knows, divided on a napkin. It is often confused with ROR (rate of return); the difference that matters is that ROR can specify a period, often a year, while ROI carries no time at all.

ROI = (Gain from Investment − Cost of Investment) ÷ Cost of Investment

Bob's sheep farm, the canonical example: $50,000 invested from the start, $70,000 in total value back — ($70,000 − $50,000) / $50,000 = 40% ROI. The formula also runs in reverse: fix the cost and a desired ROI, and it tells you the return you must produce — at $50,000 and a 40% target, exactly that $70,000.

Where ROI gets difficult

The formula is simple; the definitions aren't. For a rental property, does "cost" include capital expenditure, taxes and insurance, or just the purchase price? For a stock, is the gain taxed or not? Do cash flows in the middle count, or only the first and last numbers? Two investors can compute two honest ROIs for the same asset and both be "right" — which is why ROI comparisons only mean something when the definitions match. The deepest flaw, though, is the missing clock. A diamond at 1,000% ROI against land at 50%: the diamond wins the napkin — unless its 1,000% took 50 years and the land's 50% took a few months. ROI does its job as a base for evaluating investments; it was never meant to be the whole evaluation.

Annualized ROI restores the clock

Annualized ROI spreads the total return over the holding time, compounding-style: (returned ÷ invested)1/years − 1. Now the diamond's millennium becomes roughly 4.8% a year over 50 years (1,000% total means 11× your money: 111/50 − 1 ≈ 4.9%), and comparisons between the two calculations on this page usually find the annualized figure the more useful of the pair. Two honest caveats remain even then: risk is nowhere in either number, so lower-ROI investments are routinely and rationally preferred for being safer; and some returns can't be measured directly at all — advertising ROI is typically estimated from marginal sales lift or brand recognition rather than counted. Use ROI to screen, annualized ROI to rank, and risk to decide.

FAQ

Can ROI exceed 100%? Be negative?

Both. Getting back three times your money is a 200% ROI; getting back less than you put in is negative, bottoming at −100% for a total loss.

What about money added or taken out midway?

Plain ROI has no honest way to handle it — that's cash-flow territory. The Average Return Calculator (money-weighted) or our IRR Calculator treat dated flows properly.

Which figure should I compare, ROI or annualized?

Annualized, whenever the holding periods differ at all. Same length, same definitions — then total ROI is fine and simpler to explain.
