Payback, Discounted Payback, and the Cash Flows Beneath Them

Cash flow — the movement of cash in and out of a project over a period, usually tallied net — is the raw material of every measure on this page, and its study is a quick read on solvency: adequate cash reserves are a basic sign of financial health. Discounted cash flow (DCF) analysis goes further, restating forecast future flows in today's dollars via the time value of money; companies typically discount at their WACC, the weighted average cost of capital, because it prices the true opportunity cost of the money tied up. The discount rate itself is an inverse interest rate: it answers "what sum today, grown at this rate, reproduces those future payments?" — collapsing payments from different periods onto one comparable point in time.

The payback period

Used most in capital budgeting, the payback period is the time to the break-even point where cumulative cash flow crosses zero. A $2,000 investment returning $1,500 in year one and $500 in year two pays back in exactly two years. For an even stream it collapses to a division:

Payback Period = Initial Investment ÷ Cash Flow per Year  ·  $100 ÷ $20 = 5 years

The rule of thumb — shorter is better — survives because payback is really a risk and liquidity screen: the sooner capital is back, the less of it was ever truly at hazard. Its ease of use made it the common language of return in many boardrooms, but it ignores the time value of money entirely, so it belongs in conjunction with other metrics, never alone.

The discounted payback period

Discounted payback (DPP) repairs that flaw: break-even is computed on the NPV of the flows, so a flow counts only for what it's worth today. The first $20 of the example above is worth $20/1.10 = $18.18, the second $20/1.10² = $16.53, and so on — for a level stream the closed form is DPP = −ln(1 − I·r/CF) / ln(1 + r), giving 7.27 years against the plain 5. DPP is therefore (almost) always the longer, more honest number, and it yields a clean decision rule: if discounted payback is shorter than the asset's useful life — or any cutoff you've set — the investment at least returns its cost in present-value terms; if longer, it generally shouldn't be considered. Comparing projects, shorter DPPs are the more enticing. (The schedules above interpolate within the crossing year, so uneven flows are handled exactly rather than by formula.)

Both measures share two remaining blind spots: they say nothing about what happens after break-even (a gold mine opening in year six is invisible to them), and neither prices risk or opportunity cost directly — which is why DCF analysis and IRR, the discount rate that zeroes a project's NPV, remain the senior partners in any serious evaluation.

FAQ

Can payback be less than a year?

Yes — the within-year fraction is computed proportionally (the schedules assume each year's flow accrues evenly across that year), so a project recovering its cost seven months in shows about 0.58 years.

What if the investment never pays back?

The results say so plainly and show how much remains unrecovered at the end of the horizon — extend the years or raise the flows to see when (or whether) it crosses. A discounted stream can fail to break even even when the plain one succeeds; that's the time value of money delivering its verdict.

Average annual return — how is it defined here?

As a simple companion figure: total net profit over the horizon, divided by the years, divided by the initial investment (an ARR-style measure). It's deliberately plain — for a timing-aware rate, use IRR.
