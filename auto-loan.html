How an Auto Loan Really Adds Up

A car loan looks simple — price, payment, done — until the buyer's order adds sales tax, title and registration, a destination charge, and a "doc fee," and the monthly figure quietly stops matching the sticker. This calculator keeps every piece visible: what you finance, what you pay at signing, what the loan costs in interest, and what the car costs in total. It also runs the question backwards — give it a monthly payment and it will tell you the most expensive car that payment honestly buys at your rate and term. (Primarily a U.S. calculator; buyers elsewhere can set the tax rate to match local rules.)

Where the money comes from: direct lending vs the dealership

Direct lending is a plain loan from a bank, credit union, or online lender, arranged by you — ideally as a preapproval before you shop. It turns you into a near-cash buyer, lets you walk away from any single dealership, and forces dealers to compete on the car's price rather than on financing fog. Dealership financing is arranged at the store, usually through the manufacturer's captive lender or a bank the dealer assigns the contract to. It is convenient, and it is where subsidized rates live — manufacturers routinely advertise 0%, 0.9%, or 1.9% on new models to move them — but a dealer who controls both the price and the loan has two dials to turn instead of one. The strongest position combines both: arrive preapproved, then let the dealer try to beat your rate.

Trade-ins, rebates, and the sales-tax fine print

Your trade-in's net value — what the dealer offers minus what you still owe — is what actually reduces the deal. Owe more than it's worth and the shortfall (negative equity) is typically rolled into the new loan: you begin underwater before the new car even depreciates. On tax, most states charge only on the difference: a $50,000 car with a $10,000 trade-in at 8% is taxed ($50,000 − $10,000) × 8% = $3,200. Eight jurisdictions give no reduction — California, District of Columbia, Hawaii, Kentucky, Maryland, Michigan, Montana, Virginia — where the same deal is taxed $4,000; this calculator applies your state's rule automatically when you select it. Manufacturer rebates have their own wrinkle: about twenty states don't tax them, while the rest tax the pre-rebate price — again, the state selector handles it. Selling your old car privately usually nets more than a trade-in, and in no-reduction states it costs you nothing in tax.

The fees, itemized

Sales tax is the big one — five states (Alaska, Delaware, Montana, New Hampshire, Oregon) charge none on cars. Title and registration are the state's charges for paperwork and plates. Documentation fees pay the dealer for processing — real, but negotiable in size in some states and capped in others. Destination charges ($900–$1,500, set by the manufacturer) cover shipping the car to the lot and are legitimately part of the price. Advertising fees sometimes appear as a line item for regional marketing. And insurance: driving uninsured is illegal everywhere, lenders require full coverage on a financed car, and it can exceed $1,000 a year — budget it even though it isn't in the loan. Any fee a dealer cannot explain in one sentence is a fee to refuse. Financing taxes and fees is sometimes unavoidable, but know the price: rolled into the default loan above, the $5,500 of tax and fees would cost about $728 in extra interest over 60 months.

Strategy: term, credit, cash back, and the used-car escape

Credit decides both approval and price — excellent credit regularly borrows several points cheaper than fair credit, worth thousands over a loan — so check and polish your score before you shop, and get competing quotes. When a manufacturer offers either a cash rebate or a low rate, there is no universal winner: the rebate cuts the amount financed immediately, the low rate cuts every month's interest; the longer the term, the more the low rate is worth — run both versions through the cash back or low interest calculator and compare total cost. On term length, beware the 72- and 84-month stretch: the payment drops, but cars depreciate fastest in their first years (often 10%+ the day you drive off), so long loans spend years underwater; the classic guardrail is 20/4/10 — 20% down, four years maximum, all car costs under 10% of gross income. Paying early saves real interest where the contract allows it penalty-free — check before signing. And the cheapest new car is often a two-year-old one: someone else absorbed the steepest depreciation. Finally, the cash question: paying outright skips interest entirely (borrowing $32,000 for five years at 6% costs $5,118.98 in interest), keeps full flexibility to sell or insure as you like, and hard-caps you at a car you can actually afford — though a genuinely subsidized 0–1.9% loan can still beat cash if the money earns more elsewhere.

FAQ

How is tax figured with a trade-in?

Most states tax price minus trade-in; CA, DC, HI, KY, MD, MI, MT and VA tax the full price. Select your state above and the calculator applies the right rule.

Should taxes and fees go into the loan?

Only if cash is tight — you will pay interest on them for the full term. On the defaults, financing $5,500 of tax and fees adds about $728 of interest.

What term should I pick?

The shortest you can comfortably afford — 48–60 months is the sane range for most buyers. Long terms exist to sell payments, not cars.

I owe more than my trade-in is worth. Now what?

The shortfall rolls into the new loan (enter both numbers above and watch the loan grow). Better options: pay the gap in cash, or keep driving the old car until the balance falls below its value.
