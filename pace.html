Training through pace and heart rate

Pace measures movement; heart rate measures cost — the number of heart contractions per minute. The two rise together, and using both in training is how you improve performance without over-training: pace tells you what you did, heart rate tells you what it took.

A typical adult resting heart rate sits between 60 and 100 bpm (some argue the healthy band is really 50–90); lower usually means a more efficient heart, though anything under 50 or over 90 deserves a doctor's look. Maximum heart rate is pinned down properly only by a cardiac stress test — ten to twenty minutes of escalating effort under supervision — so most people estimate it with the most cited formula in fitness:

MHR = 220 − age

Treat that number as a sketch, not a measurement: it carries no standard deviation, and real maximums scatter widely between similar people. Still, zones built on it are useful references — an intensity of 60–70% of MHR is the classic fat-burning band, and steady aerobic work generally lives around 55–85% of MHR for 20–30 minutes at a time.

Aerobic vs. anaerobic

Aerobic exercise (roughly 70–80% of MHR) is light activity sustained for a long time: oxygen supply meets demand, and muscles produce their energy cleanly. Anaerobic exercise (roughly 80–90%) is the short, intense end — oxygen can't keep up, muscles burn sugar directly, and the byproduct, lactate, produces the familiar burn and eventually forces a stop if it isn't cleared. The pace you can hold while staying aerobic — your aerobic threshold pace — is the foundation of marathon training, because it balances fat and carbohydrate use and can be held for hours. The anaerobic (lactate) threshold is where glycogen takes over as the primary fuel and lactate starts accumulating faster than it can be removed; threshold training exists to push that point later, which is another way of saying: farther and faster before the wall.

Labs measure thresholds directly, but a good field estimate comes from a 2005 study's method: run a solo 30-minute time trial at maximum sustainable effort and average your heart rate over the last 20 minutes — that average estimates your lactate threshold heart rate (in a group, extend the trial to 60 minutes). Subtract 30 bpm and you have an estimate of your aerobic threshold heart rate.

FAQ

How do I calculate pace from time and distance?

Divide time by distance: 50:25 over 5 km is 3,025 ÷ 5 = 605 seconds per kilometer, a 10:05/km pace. The solver above also converts it to per-mile, mph, kph, meters per minute, and meters per second in one pass.

Pace or speed — which should I train with?

Runners think in pace because races are fixed distances and the question is time per kilometer. Cyclists think in speed because wind and terrain make distance-per-hour the natural readout. They are the same information, inverted — the converter above flips between them.

How accurate is the finish time projection?

It assumes you hold your current pace exactly — no fade, no kick, no hills. Real races drift, which is why the multipoint tool matters: it shows whether your segments are speeding up or slowing down, so the projection can be believed or discounted.
