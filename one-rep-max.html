What a one rep max is, and why it matters

A one-repetition maximum is the peak force your muscles can produce in one all-out effort on a specific exercise — the standard measure of maximal strength, and the number competitions are built on. It is lift-specific: bench press, squat, deadlift, and overhead press each have their own 1RM, and none of them substitutes for another. Knowing yours turns training from guesswork into programming: intensity can be prescribed as a percentage of a known max, progress becomes a number you can watch move, and a stall becomes visible early enough to fix the program rather than wonder.

Measuring it directly

Direct measurement means working up in weight, fully rested between attempts, until only one clean repetition is possible. Done properly it looks like this: warm up the muscles involved; recruit a spotter; start from a weight you can lift about 5–10 times; rest 2–5 minutes between attempts; increase in steps sized by how the last attempt felt; and stop at the last weight completed with good form — a rep finished with broken form doesn't count and is how injuries happen. Record the number. Direct testing is straightforward and the most accurate method, but it is also the most dangerous and the most time-consuming, and beginners shouldn't do it at all: early training is for learning form, and estimation (below) gives them the same planning benefits safely.

Estimation methods

Three classical formulas, all selectable in the Settings above:

Epley

1RM = W × (1 + reps / 30)

Brzycki

1RM = W × 36 / (37 − reps)

Lombardi

1RM = W × reps0.10

To feed them, pick a weight you can lift for 3–10 reps and lift it to failure — the point where another clean rep is impossible; count only completed reps with proper form. The fewer reps the set took, the better the estimate: formulas are typically within about 10% of a true max, and Epley and Brzycki — the two most popular — agree exactly at 10 reps and differ slightly elsewhere. Estimation is quicker, safer, and open to beginners and people with limitations; its weakness is that the underlying data skews toward experienced lifters, and fast-improving beginners can outgrow an estimate almost as soon as they make it.

Training with your 1RM

Percentages of 1RM target different adaptations (general guidelines — sources vary):

  • 50–60% for 3–5 reps (4–6 sets) — explosive power; keep rests short.
  • ~70% for 10–15 reps (4–6 sets) — muscular endurance.
  • 70–80% for 7–12 reps (3–5 sets) — muscle growth.
  • 80–100% for 1–3 reps (3–4 sets) — strength and power; use a spotter.

Note the table's Repetitions column tells you roughly how many reps a percentage usually allows — that is not itself a prescription. What you do at a percentage depends on the goal above.

Improving your 1RM

  • Prioritize safety. Strength comes from consistent training, and injuries are the main interruption. Guard your form — especially near your limits — and train heavy with a spotter.
  • Train consistently, at the right loads. Progress comes from working mostly at 85–100% of 1RM — but a 70% day still beats a skipped day, and real rest days are part of the program, not a failure of it.
  • Rest. Muscles grow between sessions. Constant limit-pushing — overtraining — fatigues them, blunts training quality, and invites the injury that sets you back months.
  • Vary the work when you stall. Supersets (different exercises back to back), compound sets (same muscles back to back), and pyramid sets (weight up, reps down) stress muscles in new ways; so does strengthening the supporting muscles around the main lift, which both protects you and often unlocks the plateau.

Disclaimer: consult a qualified fitness professional or healthcare provider before maximal strength testing or starting a new exercise program. Safety comes first when training for maximal strength.

FAQ

Why does the table stop at 50%?

Below half of your max, rep counts climb into territory where the formulas' assumptions stop being useful for strength programming — endurance work is prescribed by time and effort, not by 1RM percentages.

My set took 10 reps. Should I retest heavier?

If you can, yes: rest fully, add weight, and aim to fail in the 3–5 rep range. Estimates tighten considerably as the test set gets shorter.
