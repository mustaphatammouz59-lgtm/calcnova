Pensions, Defined-Benefit Plans, and the Payout Decisions

Traditionally, a pension is a fund employers contribute to as an employee benefit; at retirement the money is drawn from the pot or sold to an insurance company and distributed as periodic payments until death (a life annuity). In the U.S. the tax treatment is the attraction — preferential treatment for money placed in and for its earnings. These three calculators are designed mainly for the defined-benefit (DB) plan: the employer guarantees a defined amount at retirement regardless of investment performance, employees may or may not contribute alongside, U.S. DB plans carry no contribution limits — and the employer bears the whole obligation, which survives buyouts and restructurings, though it may mean little if the sponsor fails outright. The benefit itself is usually a formula of age, earnings history, and years of service: work longer and earn more, and the projected benefit rises. The most common DB plan in the U.S. is Social Security — designed, remember, to replace only about 40% of a worker's income.

The defined-contribution (DC) plan inverts the deal: the employer contributes defined amounts (most often a match up to a percentage of income, occasionally by years of service), the payout depends entirely on contributions plus investment performance, and there is no guaranteed benefit if assets fall. In exchange, participants control the investments and the account is portable between employers — which is why DC plans (401(k)s, IRAs, Roth IRAs) became the dominant private-sector design, and why tenure matters far less to them than to DB plans.

Why DB fell and DC rose

Too much of a DB plan's success rides on volatile factors: employees quitting or being fired, and the sponsor staying solvent for decades — the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation insures failed private pensions, but only up to limits, which is one reason surviving DB plans cluster in the public sector, where the sponsor rarely goes under. Extracting full value also demands staying ~25 years with one employer, increasingly rare; plans can be "frozen," halting further accruals, often under pressure from rising healthcare costs and longevity or unfavorable interest rates; and DB plans simply cost more to administer. The golden age of the private DB pension is, by most accounts, over.

Lump sum vs monthly, single vs joint

Most DB plans offer the choice priced in calculator 1: the monthly benefit (possibly guaranteed for life, an obligation of the employer and untouched by market volatility — but impossible to bequeath beyond a spouse) or the lump sum, whose technical name is the commuted value — the present value of the payment stream. The lump sum's flexibility cuts both ways: it can be rolled into an IRA (preserving tax deferral, and leaving anything unspent to heirs, which pension payments generally cannot do), but it can also simply be spent. Shorter life expectancy tilts toward the lump sum; a long life in good health tilts toward the checks. Calculator 2 prices the second fork: single-life pays the most and stops at death (a guarantee period of five or ten years, if offered, softens that at a lower rate); joint-and-survivor insures the spouse for their lifetime at a reduced amount, continuing after the first death at the chosen survivor benefit ratio — commonly 50%, 66%, 75%, or 100% (so a $1,000 joint pension at a 50% ratio becomes $500 for the survivor). In calculator 2 the joint amount you enter is treated as continuing in full; if your plan applies a survivor ratio, enter the amount your spouse would actually receive after your death to model the later years precisely, or weigh the difference qualitatively.

The COLA footnote

A cost-of-living adjustment keeps payouts' buying power intact as prices rise. It's standard in Social Security and common in public pensions; most private pensions have none (an overfunded plan — assets above obligations — may afford one if beneficiaries press for it; an underfunded plan usually cannot). All three calculators accept your plan's actual COLA; enter 0 if it has none, which is the honest default for much of the private sector.

FAQ

Why compare in present value instead of total dollars?

Because a dollar at 65 is worth more than a dollar at 90. Totals systematically flatter whichever option pays latest — joint pensions and delayed retirement almost always "win" on totals while sometimes losing in present value. Both figures are shown; the verdict follows present value, discounted at your investment return.

Can I change my mind after choosing?

Almost never — payout elections are typically irrevocable once payments begin, which is exactly why these comparisons are worth doing slowly, with your plan's real figures, before you sign.
