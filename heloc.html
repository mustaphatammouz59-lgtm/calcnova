What Is a HELOC?

A home equity line of credit is a revolving credit line secured by your home — closer to a credit card than to a loan. Instead of one lump sum, you get a limit you can draw against, repay, and redraw, which suits expenses that arrive in stages: a renovation paid contractor by contractor, tuition billed semester by semester. Its life splits into two very different phases, and this calculator exists because the second phase is where borrowers get surprised.

Phase 1: the draw period (interest-only)

For typically 5 to 10 years you may draw up to the limit, and most lenders require only interest payments on what you owe. That keeps payments low — $333.33 a month on a $50,000 balance at 8% — but the balance does not shrink by a cent unless you pay extra on purpose. Many HELOCs also charge a modest annual fee during this phase for keeping the line open. Treat the low payment as a minimum, not a plan: every dollar of principal you repay voluntarily during the draw period is a dollar that never enters the much more expensive second phase.

Phase 2: repayment — and the payment jump

When the draw period ends, the line closes and the remaining balance converts to an amortizing loan over the repayment period, commonly 10 to 20 years. On the default numbers here, the payment leaps from $333.33 to $477.83 — a 43% jump — on the very same balance. Budget for that jump from day one; borrowers who only ever planned around the interest-only figure are the ones who end up refinancing in a hurry or selling under pressure. Layered on top is the HELOC's defining risk: the rate is usually variable, set as an index (often prime) plus a margin, so both phases' payments can rise further if the index climbs. Every figure on this page holds today's rate constant — stress-test your budget at a rate 2 points higher.

Costs: closing costs, annual fees, and the APR

Upfront costs (application, appraisal, origination, title, document fees) typically run 1–5% of the line. "No-closing-cost" HELOCs exist, but the charge migrates into a higher margin or an early-termination fee if you close the line within a few years. With $2,000 of closing costs and a $50 annual fee, the default line's true cost rises from an 8.00% rate to an 8.56% APR — the figure to compare across lenders, since it is the only one that counts the fees. One more lender power worth knowing before you sign: if your home's value drops sharply or your finances deteriorate, the lender may freeze or reduce your line — a HELOC is flexible in both directions.

FAQ

What is the draw period?

The opening 5–10 years when you can draw, repay, and redraw up to your limit, usually paying interest only.

What happens when the draw period ends?

Draws stop and the balance amortizes over the repayment period. On $50,000 at 8%, the payment rises from $333.33 to $477.83 — plan for the jump.

Is a HELOC rate fixed?

Usually not — it floats with an index plus a margin. Some lenders let you fix a portion of the balance; ask before signing.

Can the lender freeze my line?

Yes, if the home's value falls significantly or your financial situation worsens materially. Never treat an undrawn HELOC as an emergency fund of last resort.
