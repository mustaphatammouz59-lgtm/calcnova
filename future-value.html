Future Value and the Time Value of Money

Future value (FV) is what money is expected to be worth at a later date. Cash resting in a savings account, or locked into a bond, earns compound interest — so its amount at any future checkpoint differs from today's, and FV is the arithmetic that names the checkpoint figure:

FV = PV × (1 + r)N + PMT × [((1 + r)N − 1) ÷ r]  ·  defaults: $3,108.93

The savings account is the natural classroom for the concept, because its future value simply answers "how much will be in the account then?" Try the smallest possible case in the calculator above: $10 of starting amount at 6% for 1 period, no deposits — FV comes out $10.60, ten dollars of principal and sixty cents of interest. Scale that seed up and the second term takes over: in the defaults, $1,000 of starting money and $1,000 of deposits produce $1,108.93 of pure interest — the interest ends up the largest of the three slices, which is compounding's signature. Given enough periods it always wins; the only question the inputs decide is how long "enough" takes.

One of the five keys

FV, together with PV, I/Y, N, and PMT, forms the time value of money — the backbone of finance. There could be no mortgages, auto loans, or credit cards without it: a lender computes the future value of the sum advanced, a borrower repays it in installments, and the rate is the bridge negotiated between them. FAQ

Does FV account for inflation or taxes?

Not by itself — FV is stated in nominal future dollars. For purchasing-power terms, either discount the result by expected inflation or rerun with a real (inflation-adjusted) rate; our Interest Calculator shows buying power explicitly, and the Savings Calculator can skim tax from interest as it accrues.

Can the periodic deposit be zero?

Yes — with PMT at zero the calculator reduces to pure lump-sum compounding, FV = PV × (1 + r)^N, the same engine as the Compound Interest Calculator (which adds compounding-frequency choices).
