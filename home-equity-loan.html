What Is a Home Equity Loan?

A home equity loan — often called a second mortgage — turns the equity you have built in your home into a one-time lump sum, repaid in fixed monthly payments at a fixed rate over a set term, commonly 5 to 30 years. Because your home secures the loan, rates sit well below credit cards and personal loans — but that security cuts both ways: miss payments and the lender can foreclose. The first calculator above prices a specific loan, closing costs and true APR included; the second estimates how much a lender is likely to let you borrow in the first place.

The borrowing limit: CLTV, with a worked example

Equity alone does not set your limit — the lender's combined loan-to-value cap does. Most lenders stop at 80% CLTV; stronger borrowers sometimes qualify at 85–90%. On a $600,000 home with a $250,000 mortgage, an 80% cap gives $600,000 × 80% = $480,000 of total allowed debt, minus the $250,000 already owed — $230,000 of room, exactly what the calculator above returns. Two more gates apply on top: most lenders cap home equity loans near $1,000,000 regardless of equity, and approval also weighs your credit (scores under roughly 630 often fail) and your debt-to-income ratio, which lenders generally want at or below 43% across all your debts — check it with our debt-to-income calculator.

Closing costs and why APR beats the rate

Expect 2–5% of the loan in closing costs: appraisal, origination, title search, document and notary fees. They are either deducted from your proceeds or paid in cash at closing — and they are exactly why two loans at the same rate can cost different amounts. The APR folds those costs into one annualized figure: on the default loan above, $7,500 of closing costs lifts the true cost from an 8.00% rate to an 8.86% APR. Beware the flip side of convenience: "no-closing-cost" loans simply move the charge into a higher rate or an early-payoff penalty. Compare offers on APR and total cost, never on the headline rate alone — our APR calculator goes deeper.

Prefer to draw in stages instead of taking a lump sum? Model the two-phase alternative on our HELOC calculator — it shows the interest-only draw payment and the repayment payment side by side.

One tax caution that shapes the choice for many borrowers: under current IRS rules, interest on home-equity borrowing is generally deductible only when the money goes to buy, build, or substantially improve the home itself. Interest on a loan used to consolidate credit cards usually is not. Rules change and situations differ — confirm with the current IRS guidance or a tax professional before counting on a deduction.

FAQ

How much can I borrow?

Up to your lender's CLTV cap minus what you already owe: home value × cap − mortgage balance. At 80% on a $600,000 home with a $250,000 mortgage, that is $230,000.

What closing costs should I expect?

Typically 2–5% of the loan — appraisal, origination, title, document and notary fees. On $150,000, budget roughly $3,000 to $7,500.

Home equity loan or HELOC?

Choose the loan for a fixed, known expense and payment certainty; choose a HELOC for flexible, phased draws — accepting that its variable rate can rise.

Can I lose my home?

Yes — the loan is secured by your home, so default can lead to foreclosure. Borrow only what you can repay comfortably, and keep a cash buffer.
