The 2-point rule of thumb

A 5% origination fee on a five-year loan adds about 2 points to the APR (10% becomes ≈12.2%); on a three-year loan it adds over 3. Shorter term, same fee, worse APR — fees are front-loaded costs spread over fewer payments.

Personal Loans, Up Close

A personal loan is a fixed amount, at a fixed rate, repaid in fixed monthly installments over a set term — typically $5,000 to $35,000 for three or five years in the U.S. Most are unsecured: no car or house stands behind them, so the lender prices you — credit score, income, existing debts — instead of collateral, and rates run correspondingly higher, past 25% for weaker credit. (Secured personal loans exist at banks and credit unions, backed by a car, savings, or a CD; the borrower's risk is losing the collateral.) Traditionally the lenders were banks, credit unions, and, at the expensive end, pawnshops and cash-advance stores. The internet added peer-to-peer lending: platforms match borrowers with individual investors, take a small cut as middlemen, and — because a website is cheaper than a branch network and the platform itself lends nothing — often quote better terms while the investors bear the default risk.

Why People Take Them

About half of all personal loans consolidate debt, and the arithmetic can be compelling: cards at 19.99% and 24.99% replaced by one loan whose fee-inclusive APR is 14.284% (the worked example in this calculator's lineage: $16,000 at 12% with a 5% fee) ends the balances years sooner at a lower total cost — provided the cards stay at zero afterward. The rest fund medical bills, home renovations, small-business pushes, weddings, vacations and other large purchases: anywhere a fixed payoff date beats an open-ended revolving balance.

The Fee Stack

The origination fee (1–5% typically, sometimes called an application fee) covers processing and underwriting. Most lenders deduct it — borrow $10,000 at a 3% fee and $9,700 lands in your account, while payments are computed on $10,000 — though some collect it upfront; the APR comes out the same. A prepayment fee punishes early payoff (increasingly rare, always worth checking), and a late fee punishes the opposite. Lenders may also push credit insurance — monthly premiums covering death, disability, or job loss. It is not required by law; priced against term life insurance and an emergency fund, it is usually poor value, and this calculator shows exactly what it adds to the APR.

Creditworthiness, Applications, and Predators

Your credit profile is the main pricing input: strong scores unlock the low rates; thin or damaged credit means few offers at punishing prices, and defaulting damages the score further. Lenders that look past scores weigh debt-to-income ratio and employment stability instead. Applications are straightforward — identity, employment, income, and credit information, evidenced by tax returns, pay stubs or W-2s, increasingly submitted online with decisions in minutes to days and funding sometimes within 24 hours by direct deposit. One warning deserves emphasis: a lender who offers money without checking credit history is a red flag, not a favor. Loans marketed by mail or phone, auto-title loans, cash advances and payday loans cluster at the predatory end — extreme rates, heavy fees, brutally short terms. Before signing anything unsecured, price the alternatives: family or friends, a cosigner with strong credit, a 0% introductory-rate card used with discipline, a secured loan against existing collateral, community nonprofits — even crowdfunding, which needs no repayment at all.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the start date change my payment?

No — it only labels the schedule and sets the payoff date. The payment depends on amount, rate and term; the calendar decides when each installment falls.

Why does the schedule's last payment fall a month before the payoff date?

The payoff date is quoted as start date plus the full term (an October 2026 start on a five-year loan pays off "Oct. 2031"), while the 60th monthly payment itself lands in September 2031. Lenders quote both ways; the money is identical.

Can I use this for a secured personal loan?

Yes — the arithmetic is the same. Just remember the calculator can't price the risk that matters most with secured borrowing: losing the collateral if payments stop.
