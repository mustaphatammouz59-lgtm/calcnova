Maintenance is the pivot

Every weight plan is arithmetic around one number: eat at your TDEE and you stay put, roughly 250-500 Calories under it loses about a quarter to half a kilo a week, and the same margin over it gains. TDEE is an estimate, so adjust from what your weight actually does over two or three weeks.

What is TDEE?

TDEE — total daily energy expenditure — is all the energy you use in a day. It is genuinely hard to measure and shifts from day to day, so in practice it is estimated from three components. Basal metabolic rate (BMR) is your energy use at complete rest in a temperate environment with the digestive system idle: the minimum needed to run the vital organs. Activity level covers everything you do on top of that — deliberate exercise (the thermic effect of activity) plus the movement of work and daily life, known as non-exercise activity thermogenesis, or NEAT. The thermic effect of food is the energy cost of processing what you eat, often estimated at about 10% of intake, though it varies by food — protein, for instance, costs far more to process than fat.

How is TDEE calculated?

The calculation starts with BMR, estimated by an equation built for the purpose from your age, gender, height, and weight. The common ones are the Mifflin St Jeor equation, the Harris-Benedict equation, and the Katch-McArdle formula; they land close to each other, with Katch-McArdle holding an edge for lean people because it works from lean body mass rather than total weight. This calculator offers Mifflin St Jeor and Katch-McArdle in Settings. The BMR is then multiplied by an activity factor reflecting exercise and how active your job and daily life are. The thermic effect of food can be layered on as well, but it is comparatively small next to BMR and activity, and is not always included — here it is left inside the margin of the estimate rather than added separately.
