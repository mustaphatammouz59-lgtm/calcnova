Rebate or Rate: Pricing the Two Offers Honestly

The choice looks like marketing — a pile of cash or a tiny rate — but it is arithmetic. The rebate shrinks the loan on day one; the low rate shrinks every month's interest for the life of the loan. Which wins depends on just four things: the size of the rebate, the gap between the two rates, the amount financed, and the term. Bigger loans and longer terms multiply the value of a rate cut; small loans and short terms make the cash worth more. The verdict above is decided the honest way — total cost of the whole deal under each offer, tax and fees included — and the break-even rebate shown with it tells you exactly how large a rebate would have to be to flip the answer. (Tax and fee treatment follows U.S. rules; buyers elsewhere can set the tax rate to match.)

The cash rebate, up close

Manufacturer rebates run from a few hundred to several thousand dollars and exist mainly to move metal — clearing last year's inventory or jump-starting a slow seller. Alongside the headline rebate sit narrower ones: military, recent-graduate, first-time-buyer, loyalty for returning same-brand owners, and "conquest" money for switching from a rival. Some require financing through the manufacturer's captive lender. Watch the tax line: in about twenty states a rebate is treated as the manufacturer's money, so sales tax is figured on the price after the rebate; in the rest, you are taxed on the full price as if the rebate never happened — this calculator applies your state's rule when you select it. Rebates arrive either as an instant deduction at signing (best) or a mail-in check weeks later. And do not confuse a rebate with holdback — the 2–3% of MSRP the manufacturer quietly returns to the dealer; the rebate is yours, the holdback is theirs, and the car's price is negotiable on top of both.

Low-interest financing, up close

A subsidized rate — 0%, 0.9%, 1.9% — is the manufacturer buying down your interest through its captive lender, and it is usually reserved for "well-qualified buyers": excellent credit, often a healthy down payment, and frequently a shorter term than the rebate deal allows. Two cautions. First, some low rates are introductory, lasting 12 months before resetting higher; this calculator assumes the rate holds for the whole term, so it does not fit teaser deals. Second, the rate's value evaporates if you pay the loan off early or trade the car in year two — you only collect interest savings for the months you actually carry the balance. Financing through the dealer for the rate also hands the dealer both dials (price and loan) at once; arrive with an outside preapproval so the "low rate" has something to beat.

Considerations before you sign either way

Shop the rate anyway. Even the manufacturer's best offer may lose to your bank or credit union, especially on used cars where captive lenders rarely play. Mind the term being sold with the rate. Loans have stretched to 84–90 months; pairing a long term with fast depreciation is how buyers end up underwater — owing more than the car is worth. The low rate paired with a short term is the genuinely cheap deal; check both columns' totals, not just the monthly payment. Keep negotiating the car. A rebate comes from the manufacturer, not the dealer's pocket, so accepting it does not end the negotiation over the car's price. Beware the bait. Advertising a 0% rate that most visitors mysteriously "don't qualify" for is an old, illegal-in- most-places trick that still works because by the time the rate changes, the buyer has fallen for the car. And let your own situation vote: a buyer whose credit will not qualify for the low rate, or who needs liquidity for something urgent, should take the cash without a second thought — the calculator prices money, not circumstances.

FAQ

So which is better?

Whichever removes more money in total. On the defaults, the drop from 5% to 2% saves $3,092 in interest — more than the $1,000 rebate, so the low rate wins. A rebate above $3,092 would flip it.

Are rebates taxed?

Depends on the state — about twenty don't tax them (tax on the reduced price); the rest tax the full price. The state selector applies the right rule.

Who gets the low rate?

"Well-qualified buyers" — excellent credit, sometimes more money down or a shorter term. If your credit is bruised, the rebate is likely the only real offer.

Paying cash for the car?

Take the rebate, always. A rate you never pay is worth zero.
